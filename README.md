# CS 250 Portfolio Artifact: Sprint Review and Retrospective

Welcome to my CS 250 portfolio submission. This repository contains the Sprint Review and Retrospective deliverable from my final project, showcasing the Agile and Scrum practices applied throughout this course. 

Below are my reflections on the essential questions of the Computer Science program.

### Interpreting User Needs and the Role of User Stories
Interpreting user needs requires active listening, empathy, and the ability to translate vague business requests into concrete technical requirements. I approach this by focusing on the underlying problem the user is trying to solve rather than just the features they ask for. Creating "user stories" is instrumental in this process because it forces the development team to view the software from the end-user's perspective. By framing requirements as *"As a [type of user], I want [a goal] so that [a reason],"* we ensure that the "why" behind the code is never lost, keeping the development focused on delivering actual value.

### Approaching Program Development and Future Agile Practices
My approach to developing programs is highly iterative, favoring continuous feedback and incremental improvements over massive, monolithic releases. Moving forward in my career, I hope to deeply incorporate core Agile processes such as sprint planning, daily stand-ups, and rigorous sprint retrospectives. I also plan to maintain a highly prioritized and groomed product backlog. Working in IT, I've seen how quickly priorities can shift; utilizing Agile frameworks allows a team to remain flexible, adapt to changing user needs, and continuously deliver working software without burning out.

### Being a Good Team Member in Software Development
To me, being a good team member in software development means balancing technical collaboration with strong, transparent communication. It involves writing clean, well-documented code that is easy for others to read, participating constructively in code reviews, and being open to feedback without taking it personally. A good team member also supports their peers—whether that means helping unblock a teammate who is stuck on a bug or stepping up to handle a task when the sprint goal is at risk. Ultimately, it means prioritizing the success of the team and the quality of the product over individual ego.
